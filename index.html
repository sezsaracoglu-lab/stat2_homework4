How to read a Theme 4 question

Twenty-two questions, seven underlying tasks. This page teaches you to name the task before you touch a formula.

Sezen — how this one differs from Theme 5

Theme 5 was arithmetic wearing a story. You found n and p, picked a formula, and computed. Theme 4 is not like that. Only about a quarter of these questions have a calculation in them at all. The rest are testing whether you can say, in a sentence, what a number means — and whether you can spot a sentence that sounds right and isn't.

So the opening move is different. In Theme 5 you looked for numbers. Here you look for a verb. Does the question say interpret, conclude, predict, compute, or which is true? That verb, not the topic, tells you which of the seven recipes you are on.

One more thing worth saying plainly, because it is the spine of the whole theme. Every warning in this chapter exists because the numbers cannot see the picture. r, the slope, r² and the p-value are all computed identically whether your data are a tidy straight band, a banana, or a blob with one wild point in the corner. Roughly half of these 22 questions are that idea in one costume or another.

🧭 The router: find the verb, find the recipe

Read the last sentence of the question. The word it uses tells you everything.

Seven destinations. The verb in the last sentence picks one almost every time — when it does not, check the trigger phrases at the top of each recipe.

📖 A worked example of the method itself

Question 6, read the way I want you to read all of them.

The question says: “A chemist was conducting an experiment to find how many mLs of a particular substance dissolve in different temperatures of water. A correlation of 0.87 was computed. Which interpretation is TRUE?”

  1. Find the verb. “Which interpretation…” — so this is about what a number means, not about computing a new one.
  2. Find the number and its name. “A correlation of 0.87.” The word correlation means that 0.87 is r, not r².
  3. Scan the options for the phrase that names a different quantity. Two of them say “% of the variation explained”. That phrase is r², never r.
  4. Do the one piece of arithmetic. 0.87² = 0.7569 → 76%.
  5. Pick the option that matches. Done. The chemistry never mattered; the word “correlation” did all the work.

Notice the shape of that. The whole question turned on one word — whether 0.87 was called a correlation or a coefficient of determination. Theme 4 is full of questions like this, where the answer is decided by a single noun in the stem.

★ The habit to build

Underline the quantity the question names, and the quantity the options name. If they are different — “correlation” in the stem, “variation explained” in the options — you have found the question. Half of Theme 4 is a translation exercise between r, r², the slope and a prediction.

🍳 The seven recipes

Theme 4 has far less arithmetic than Theme 5 and far more judgement. Each recipe below is a way of thinking, with the one or two formulas it needs attached. The pills at the bottom of each jump to the questions that use it.

Recipe 1 · used in questions 1, 3

🏷️ Name the roles, and name the direction

Which variable explains which, and which way do they move together?

🔍 Trigger phrases. “The explanatory variable is…” · “Are the following two variables positively associated, negatively associated or not associated?”
The direction question is a reading test, not a statistics test. Slow down on the nouns.
The steps
  1. Explanatory or response? The response is what you are trying to account for; the explanatory variable is what you account for it with. Explanatory on x, response on y.
  2. When in doubt, ask which one could sensibly be changed first. Engine size is fixed when a car is built; the mileage follows.
  3. For direction: write both variables out as sentences, then imagine x rising. Does y rise with it, or fall?
  4. Watch for variables that are two halves of one total — biscuits eaten and biscuits left, gas in the tank and cost to fill it. Those are always negative.
🔁 Carries to: everything. Once you have fixed which variable is y, the slope, the residual and the whole regression follow. Get this wrong at the start and every later step is wrong too.

Recipe 2 · used in questions 4, 5, 9

👁️ Read r off a picture

Estimating a correlation by eye, and knowing what a plot can and cannot tell you.

🔍 Trigger phrases. “From this plot, the correlation … is” · “Which of the following would be a valid conclusion from this plot?” · any question that shows you a scatterplot and no equation.
Illustrative clouds, drawn to calibrate the eye. The two red boxes are the only two ways a “conclusion” option is ever wrong.
The steps
  1. Sign first. Does the cloud rise or fall from left to right? That fixes the sign before you think about size.
  2. Then tightness. A band you could almost draw a line through → 0.9 or more. A visible drift with lots of scatter → about 0.5. A shapeless blob → near 0.
  3. For “valid conclusion” questions: for each option, ask “could I point at this on the plot?” If yes, it is valid. If it needs a future year, or a reason, it is not.
  4. Watch for n = 2. Two points always lie exactly on a line, so |r| = 1 — unless the two x’s or the two y’s are identical, when r does not exist at all. Either way the number means nothing about the world.
🔁 Carries to: every plot you will ever be shown. The question “could I point at this?” separates description from speculation, and that is the entire difference between a valid and an invalid conclusion.

Recipe 3 · used in questions 2, 6, 10, 11

🥧 r, r², and which one measures what

The most heavily tested distinction in Theme 4, and the easiest to get wrong under pressure.

🔍 Trigger phrases. “% of the variation … is explained” · “which indicates a stronger relationship?” · “which measures only the strength?” · “the sign of r and the slope”.
If you can fill in this table from memory, four questions in this homework are free.
The steps
  1. Read the noun in the stem. “A correlation of 0.87” means that number is r. If the options talk about “% of the variation”, they mean r², so you must square it.
  2. “Stronger?” → compare |r|. The sign is direction, never size.
  3. “Only the strength?” → r². r carries direction in its sign; the slope carries direction and units.
  4. Sign of r vs sign of b₁? Always the same, because b₁ = r · s(y)/s(x) and standard deviations cannot be negative.
🔁 Carries to: the reverse question. Given r² and told the line slopes downwards, r = −√r² — you need the slope or the plot to put the sign back, because r² threw it away.

⚠ The trap

Quoting r as a percentage. “r = 0.87, so 87% of the variation is explained” is the single most common error in this chapter, and Q6 plants it as an option. The phrase “of the variation” is your alarm: whenever you see it, square first.

Recipe 4 · used in questions 8, 12, 14, 15, 20

📐 Use the line: slope, prediction, residual

The arithmetic third of the homework, plus the sentence that has to go with it.

🔍 Trigger phrases. “The slope of the regression line indicates…” · “which description best represents the value of the slope?” · “What is the predicted…” · “What is the residual…” · “we conclude that for every…”.
ŷ = b₀ + b₁x  ·  residual = observed y − predicted ŷ and the sentence: “one unit up in x predicts b₁ units of change in y, on average”
Every slope-interpretation question in the homework is graded on these four parts. Say all four and you cannot be caught.
The steps
  1. Identify x and y from the equation. Whatever is multiplied by the coefficient is x; whatever the equation is equal to is y.
  2. For a slope sentence: say all four parts — one unit up in x, b₁ units in y, the direction from the sign, and on average.
  3. To predict: substitute x and compute. Nothing more.
  4. For a residual: observed − predicted. Negative means the case sits below the line.
  5. From computer output: the predictor's row gives b₁; the Constant row gives b₀. Never swap them.
🔁 Carries to: Theme 5 unchanged. The same four-part sentence, the same substitution, the same observed minus predicted. The only thing Theme 5 adds is a standard error next to each b.

Recipe 5 · used in questions 13, 19

✅ What least squares guarantees — and what it doesn't

The true/false questions, and the one about computer output.

🔍 Trigger phrases. “Which of the following statements … is/are TRUE?” with numbered statements I, II, III · “We can conclude from this information that…”.
Sort every statement onto one of the two lists. If it is on the right, no amount of output can establish it.
The steps
  1. Take each numbered statement separately and decide true or false before you look at the answer options. Then match.
  2. Watch for a statement that is another one with a word reversed — “minimises” becoming “maximises”. Two statements that contradict each other cannot both be true, which usually eliminates half the options at once.
  3. For computer output: a big R² and a tiny p-value show the line fits. They cannot show the line was the right shape.
  4. What is missing from a printout is always the scatterplot and the residual plot. If the answer “we don't have enough information” is offered, ask whether those are what's missing.
🔁 Carries to: Theme 5, where “the model fits quite well and we have verified the validity of the model” is offered again — and is false for exactly this reason. It is the same idea two homeworks running.

Recipe 6 · used in questions 7, 18

🚧 Where the line stops working

Extrapolation, and the one point that runs the whole fit.

🔍 Trigger phrases. A value of x far outside the data (“in the year 3000”, “a 40-year-old”) · a circled point on a plot · “deleting it should…”.
Both failures are invisible in r, in the slope and in r². Only the plot and the range of x reveal them.
The steps
  1. For a prediction question, check the x value against the data range first. If it is far outside, the honest answer is that no prediction is justified — even when the arithmetic runs perfectly.
  2. For a circled point: is it extreme in x? If yes it has leverage and is influential. If it is only unusual in y, it is an outlier and moves the line very little.
  3. Which way would deleting it move the slope? Picture which end of the line it is pulling, and which way. Removing it lets that end spring back.
  4. Note that a point can be perfectly ordinary in y and still run the fit — influence comes from x, not from y.
🔁 Carries to: Theme 5's residual plots. Curvature, influence and extrapolation are three faces of the same fact — a summary number cannot tell you whether it was entitled to exist.

Recipe 7 · used in questions 16, 17, 21, 22

🔗 Correlation is not causation

Four questions, four different names for the same problem.

🔍 Trigger phrases. “One can reasonably conclude that…” · “the variables … are ______ variables” · “what is the best way to establish … cause-and-effect?” · any question where averages replace individuals.
Lurking: z drives both, no arrow between x and y. Confounded: x and z both act on y, tangled together.
The steps
  1. Ask what else could produce this pattern. Usually it is something obvious — city size, the size of the fire, how hot the day was.
  2. Reject any option that invents a mechanism the data cannot show (“men spend time at the ballpark”).
  3. But also reject “it's just a coincidence.” The pattern is usually real and repeatable — it is the causal reading that fails, not the correlation.
  4. That combination is why “none of the above” is worth taking seriously here — the two obvious answers are wrong in opposite directions.
  5. Averages instead of individuals? That is an ecological correlation, and it will be larger than the individual one.
  6. “How do we establish cause?” → an experiment. Never a bigger sample, never r², never a residual plot.
🔁 Carries to: your work. Any dashboard showing two organisational measures moving together is this picture, and “what third thing drives both?” is almost always the right first question.

🎴 The cheat card

If you memorise nothing else from Theme 4, memorise this page.

① The opening move

Read the last sentence and find the verb: name · read · interpret · compute · conclude. Then ask the one question that sorts every Theme 4 item: is this about the numbers, or about the picture?

② r, r² and the slope — the table to memorise

r · −1 to +1 · direction ✓ strength ✓ · no units
r² · 0 to 1 · direction ✗ strength ✓ · a share of the variation
slope b₁ · any value · direction ✓ strength ✗ · carries the units
Only r² measures strength and nothing else. And r and b₁ always share a sign, because b₁ = r · s(y)/s(x).

③ The five formulas

b₁ = r · s(y)/s(x) — the response’s SD on top
b₀ = ȳ − b₁x̄ — because (x̄, ȳ) is always on the line
ŷ = b₀ + b₁x — substitute and read off
residual = observed y − predicted ŷ — negative means the point lies below the line
r² = the fraction of the variation in y explained by the regression on x

④ Saying a slope, in four parts

One unit up in x · b₁ units of change in y · the direction from the sign · on average.
Hunt for “on average” in the option, and reject “for any student”, “each one will”, “always”.

⑤ Reading a plot

Sign first (rises or falls), then tightness. ±0.3 a drift · ±0.5 loose but visible · ±0.9 a clear band · ±0.99 almost a line.
For a “valid conclusion”: could I point at this on the plot? If it needs a future year or a reason, it is not valid.

⑥ Where the line stops working

Extrapolation: check x against the range of the data before substituting. The equation never warns you.
Influence comes from x, not from y. An extreme x has leverage; an odd y in the middle is just an outlier.
A high R² shows the line fits — not that a line was the right shape. Only a residual plot can show that.

⑦ Correlation is not causation — four names

Lurking variable: z drives both x and y; no arrow between them (baseball teams and divorces — city size).
Confounding: x and z both act on y, tangled together (vitamin C and exercise).
Ecological correlation: group averages instead of people → r goes up.
The only fix: a well-designed experiment. Never a bigger sample, never more arithmetic.
